## The real problem

Specific professional audiences endure painful, recurring workflow problems that are too narrow for funded startups to pursue, too small for enterprise software to serve well, and too specific for generic tools to address. These are the frictions that force professionals into clumsy workarounds, expensive overkill tools, or tedious manual steps — pain they tolerate because no one has built the right tool, not because the pain is trivial. The opportunity is to find the single strongest such problem and build the focused web tool that eliminates it.

The founder's constraint is that this discovery must happen before 12 weeks and $5,000 are committed to building. The problem is not coding — it is finding the right problem, one where pain intensity, audience reachability, solo-build feasibility, and revenue proximity converge, then executing end-to-end to a working MVP and a live, paying beta. Every wrong exploration costs time the 12-week clock does not refund; every safe-but-shallow choice wastes a solo founder's one structural advantage — the ability to go narrow and specific where teams cannot afford to. The operator has asked for the single strongest opportunity, not the safest.

## Who feels it

The solo technical founder who is the operator. The raw intent names no secondary buyer, user persona, industry, or market segment, and none is invented here. A specific professional audience is required — the specificity is pinned — but which audience remains genuinely open and must be discovered through ideation, not pre-assigned. The audience must be specific enough that someone could find and interview ten of them next week, and a first user can be named.

The founder's own domain expertise, network, and industry access are unknown; they are not supplied and must not be assumed. Yet whatever access the founder actually possesses is the most plausible wedge-finding lever and the most decisive filter in narrowing from all professional audiences to the ones this founder can credibly reach, understand, and sell to. Whether the founder has deep access to a particular profession or is starting cold is unknown — but audience reachability, given the founder's actual position, is a first-order selection criterion, not a question to defer. A tool the founder cannot get in front of its users will fail regardless of how well it solves the problem.

## Why now

The operator has imposed a 12-week schedule and a $5,000 budget, creating a hard temporal and financial frame for the build. No external market timing — regulatory shift, technology disruption, sector trend, or competitive vacuum — is supplied or claimed for any opportunity. The urgency is operator-generated: the decision to act now with bounded resources. If a genuine timing advantage surfaces for a specific concept during ideation, it must come from evidence the process uncovers, not from an assertion planted in the brief.

## The wedge

One painful, recurring workflow problem. One specific professional audience. One focused web tool — not a platform, not a suite, not a feature collection. The operator has pinned the shape: B2B SaaS, niche, single problem, web medium, solo-buildable, 12-week schedule, $5,000 budget, working MVP, and a live, paying beta where users pay during the beta period. The substance — which audience, which problem, which mechanic — is entirely open and is the prize of divergent thinking.

The ideation process must find where audience specificity, pain intensity, recurrence frequency, solo-build feasibility, revenue proximity, and audience reachability intersect. The 12-week / $5K constraint is a build-phase filter that eliminates concepts requiring multi-person teams, long sales cycles, heavy infrastructure, regulatory clearance, or enterprise procurement — but it is a phase boundary, not the ceiling of the opportunity. The strongest wedge may be one where the initial build produces a working MVP and paying beta that is also the foundation of a durable micro-asset: a tool with switching costs, a niche too small for funded competitors but big enough to sustain a solo founder, and a pain recurring enough that revenue recurs with it.

The wedge must be defensible, not merely useful. A derivative concept that an incumbent could absorb as a weekend feature is a failure of imagination, not a prudent choice. The boldest wedge might be a contrarian take on how the work actually gets done, a cross-domain transplant of a proven mechanic from one profession into another where it has no precedent, or an unserved segment whose pain is dismissed as "just how it is" by everyone except the people who live with it. The brief must not pre-judge the 10× version out of the frame: a deliberately too-ambitious swing that fits the constraints is a serious answer, not a reckless one.

## The bet

That within the unconstrained space of professional audiences and their recurring workflow problems, there exists at least one where: the pain is acute and frequent enough that professionals would pay during a beta; a solo technical founder can build a working MVP in the available time and budget without assuming capabilities beyond "solo technical"; evidence can distinguish the strongest concept from weaker ones before commitment; the audience is reachable by this founder through whatever channels the founder's actual position affords; and a credible path to first revenue exists — whether through the founder's existing network, domain credibility, organic reach, or a distribution channel the concept itself creates. The bet is that the strongest answer may be one the operator has not yet imagined — a contrarian niche too narrow for a funded startup to bother with, a cross-domain transplant that brings a workflow pattern from one profession into another, or a deliberately unambitious-sounding tool that solves a problem everyone has learned to tolerate but no one has solved well. The bet is that the right wedge is not the safest build but the one most defensible against incumbent absorption and most durable beyond the first transaction — a micro-asset whose value compounds rather than a launch that spikes and fades. Every part of this bet will be tested during ideation, and the idea that survives evidence-based elimination must leave the founder with the highest conviction possible under the constraints.

## What downstream must answer

**Which audience and which problem.** No role, industry, or vertical is named. No pain point is asserted. The audience must be specific enough that a first user exists and can be named, and reachable enough that the founder can get the tool in front of them. The founder's domain expertise, network, and prior experience are unknown and must not be assumed; whatever the founder actually possesses is the decisive filter, and the ideation process must surface it rather than presuppose it.

**What counts as evidence.** The operator requires evidence-based elimination of weak concepts but does not define "evidence." Expert interviews, public data analysis, competitor teardowns, landing-page smoke tests, and founder judgment grounded in personal experience are all candidate methods, each producing a different elimination filter. The ideation process must either select its evidence standard or surface the question for operator clarification.

**Scope of the $5,000.** Whether the budget covers only build and hosting or also includes customer acquisition, paid tools, outsourced design, or services is unspecified. If acquisition spend is in scope, the addressable audience shrinks to those reachable through organic or near-zero-cost channels.

**Revenue timeline and path beyond beta.** "A live, paying beta" pins that users pay during the beta period. "A clear path to first revenue" is required but its relationship to the beta payments — whether first revenue means the beta itself or the first non-beta customers that follow — and how revenue scales beyond the initial build, remain for the ideation process to resolve per concept.

**Meaning of "runtime proof."** Whether this means a deployed app with real users and real data or a functional demonstration with staged examples affects how much budget is reserved for deployment and onboarding versus core feature build, which in turn constrains how ambitious the concept can be.

**Technology stack.** "Web tool" pins the medium. No framework, language, hosting provider, database, or third-party service is named or assumed.

**Pricing model and price point.** "Paying beta" and "SaaS" are pinned. Whether pricing is per-seat, flat, usage-based, or tiered — and at what dollar amount — is open.

**Go-to-market channel.** No acquisition strategy, distribution channel, or sales motion is named. Reachability is a first-order wedge criterion, but the specific channel is open and must be part of the idea, not an afterthought.

**Competitive positioning.** No incumbent, substitute, or reference point is named. Competitors may surface for specific concepts during ideation, but no competitive landscape is pre-assigned.

**Company size, segment, and geography.** B2B is pinned. Whether the audience consists of solo professionals, SMB, mid-market, or enterprise — and in what locale — is entirely open.
